### What does the company actually do?

Modern electronic products contain chips that perform calculations, process signals, store information, and control hardware. Before these chips are manufactured, engineers must design and thoroughly test their functionality.

Edveon works in this chip-development ecosystem.

For example, imagine a chip designed to control a traffic signal. Engineers need to check that:

* Red, yellow, and green lights follow the correct sequence.

* Two conflicting directions never receive a green signal simultaneously.

* The controller behaves correctly after reset.

* It handles unusual input conditions without failing.

A design verification engineer creates tests to check these behaviors before the chip is manufactured.

The key idea: RTL design builds the digital hardware logic; design verification checks whether that logic behaves correctly.

A simplified chip development flow looks like this:

1. Specification

Define what the chip must do.

2. RTL Design

Describe digital logic using Verilog or SystemVerilog.

3. Functional Verification

Apply test cases and check expected behavior against actual outputs.

4. Synthesis and Implementation

Translate RTL into a gate-level design and implement the chip.

5. Manufacturing and Silicon Testing

Fabricate the chip, then test and validate the physical device.

This is a simplified overview, not a strict sequence for every project. Verification happens at several stages, and findings can send a design back for correction. ### What does a design verification trainee do?

A typical verification task might involve a four-bit counter.

The designer creates a counter that should count from 0 to 15. You would:

1. Understand the specification.

2. Create or run a testbench.

3. Apply clock and reset signals.

4. Check whether the counter increments correctly.

5. Check what happens at 15, after reset, and under unusual conditions.

6. Investigate failures and report bugs.

The important distinction is that verification checks whether the design meets its specification, rather than merely checking whether the code compiles.

## 5. Technical subjects to study, in priority order

For a fresher targeting design verification, I recommend this order. It is a preparation roadmap based on the role, not an official Edveon examination syllabus.

## 1

Digital Electronics: your highest priority

Study thoroughly:

* Number systems: binary, decimal, hexadecimal, signed numbers and two's complement.

* Logic gates: AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR, XOR and XNOR.

* Boolean algebra, De Morgan's laws and simplification.

* Combinational circuits: half adder, full adder, multiplexers, demultiplexers, encoders and decoders.

* Sequential circuits: SR, JK, D and T flip-flops.

* Latches versus flip-flops.

* Registers, shift registers and counters.

* Clock, reset, setup time, hold time and propagation delay.

* Finite state machines: Moore and Mealy models.

Interview goal: draw the circuit, explain its truth table and solve simple design problems without relying on memorization.

## 2

Verilog HDL

Learn:

* Module declarations, inputs, outputs and internal signals.

* `wire`, `reg` and `logic` concepts.

* `assign`, `always`, `always_comb` and `always_ff`.

* `if-else`, `case` and loops.

* Blocking (`=`) versus nonblocking (`<=`) assignments.

* Combinational versus sequential logic.

* Clock and reset behavior.

* Writing synthesizable RTL.

Interview goal: write basic Verilog for a mux, adder, D flip-flop, counter and simple FSM.

## 3

SystemVerilog fundamentals

Learn:

* Data types and arrays.

* Tasks and functions.

* Testbench structure.

* Clock generation and reset sequences.

* Assertions at a basic level.

* Random stimulus and constrained random concepts.

* Interfaces and packages at an introductory level.

SystemVerilog is important because Edveon explicitly lists SV and UVM-based verification services.

![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.edveon.com\&sz=32)

Edveon

## 4

Functional verification and UVM

Start with the fundamentals:

* Design Under Test (DUT).

* Testbench, stimulus and expected output.

* Directed tests and corner cases.

* Scoreboard and reference model.

* Functional coverage.

* Assertions.

* Basic UVM concepts: sequence, sequencer, driver, monitor, agent, environment and scoreboard.

Do not try to memorize the whole UVM framework before understanding how a simple testbench works.

## 5

Computer architecture and timing

Study CPU basics, ALU, registers, memory, buses, pipelining, synchronous design, clock domains, setup/hold violations and the difference between simulation and synthesis.

## 6

C programming and problem-solving

Revise variables, data types, operators, conditions, loops, functions, arrays, strings, pointers and bitwise operators. Practice binary manipulation and simple logic problems.

C is useful for programming fundamentals and is mentioned alongside SystemVerilog and UVM in Edveon's verification services.

![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.edveon.com\&sz=32)

Edveon

### What about analog electronics and communication?

As an ECE student, revise basic circuit laws, diodes, transistors, op-amps, ADC/DAC and sampling concepts. These are useful for general technical interviews, especially if the specific role involves mixed-signal verification or validation.

However, for a digital Design Verification Trainee role, prioritize digital electronics and HDL before spending significant time on advanced analog or communication theory.

## 8. A 14-day preparation plan

If you are starting with limited Verilog experience, follow this sequence. Spend around 2 to 3 focused hours per day if your college schedule permits.

Days 1–2

Number systems and logic gates

Binary conversions, Boolean algebra, truth tables and logic simplification.

Days 3–4

Combinational circuits

Half adder, full adder, mux, decoder, encoder and comparator.

Days 5–6

Sequential circuits

Latches, flip-flops, registers, counters, clock and reset.

Days 7–8

Verilog basics

Write modules for a mux, adder and D flip-flop; understand assignment types.

Days 9–10

Testbench and simulation

Create testbenches, run test cases and inspect waveforms.

Days 11–12

SystemVerilog and verification

DUT, stimulus, scoreboard, assertions and introductory UVM concepts.

Day 13

Interview and resume preparation

Revise projects, prepare your introduction and practise explaining your code.

Day 14

Mock interview

Solve digital logic problems, write short RTL modules and answer technical questions aloud.

If the interview is sooner, focus on digital electronics, basic Verilog, and explaining one or two projects well. Learn advanced UVM topics progressively rather than rushing through them.
